Funding announcements are written to impress. The headline number is real, but on its own it tells
you very little about whether a company is healthy, whether its tools will still exist in three
years, or who ultimately pays for them. Here is how we read one, using an invented example.

## The announcement

"Example Labs raises $400M Series C at a $3.2B valuation to accelerate its AI coding platform."

  <KeyNumber value="$3.2B" label="Post-money valuation" note="Up from $900M at Series B" />
  <KeyNumber value="~40x" label="Implied revenue multiple" note="On reported $80M ARR" />

## Step 1: Separate the valuation from the cash

The $3.2B is a price agreed between the company and its newest investors for a small slice of the
business. Nobody has $3.2B. The $400M is the cash that actually arrives and has to fund
operations, and most of it will be spent on compute and salaries.

## Step 2: Work out the burn

Companies rarely publish burn rate, but you can bracket it. If the previous round was 18 months
ago and this one is described as giving "three years of runway", the company expects to spend
roughly $130M a year. For a business with $80M in annual recurring revenue, that means it is still
losing money on every customer it serves, which is normal at this stage but worth knowing.

## Step 3: Ask what the multiple assumes

A 40x multiple only makes sense if investors expect revenue to grow several-fold while the company
keeps most of its customers. That is a bet on both the product and on the underlying model costs
falling. If compute prices stay flat, the margin story does not work.

## Step 4: Follow the strategic money

Look at *who* invested. When a cloud provider or model vendor leads a round, part of the "cash" is
often credits redeemable only on that vendor's platform. The company's independence, and its
customers' ability to switch, shrinks accordingly.

<Callout title="Why we care" tone="info">
Our interest is not in picking winners. It is in understanding whether the tools people come to
rely on, for coding, creative work or accessibility, are built on business models that can survive
without locking users in or quietly raising prices once alternatives disappear.
</Callout>

## What we would watch next

- Whether pricing changes in the twelve months after the round.
- Whether the company publishes usage limits or model-provider dependencies.
- Whether the next round is up, flat or down, which will say more than any press release.
